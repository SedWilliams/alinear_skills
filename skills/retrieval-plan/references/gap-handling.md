# Gap handling

What counts as a gap, how to classify one, and the fix for each kind. References are at the end.

## What counts as a gap

1. Anything the learner struggled to recall: slow, partial, or needed a cue.
2. Anything they got wrong.
3. Anything they felt unsure about, even when the answer was right.

Gaps aren't only wrong answers. Getting one version of a question right doesn't mean getting its variations right, and correct answers given without confidence are less likely to come back later (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2008).

## Where to aim

- Start with the areas the learner feels least sure of or hasn't used before, even when that's uncomfortable. Those are also the easiest to skip.
- Big-picture gaps before small details.
- Open gaps from the last session before new territory.
- Watch for blind spots. A learner who doesn't know enough to see what their testing missed can carry a gap through several sessions. An outside check catches it.

## Classify each gap

| Axis | Values | How to tell |
|---|---|---|
| Level | Bloom 1–6 | What the question demanded: recall a fact (1), explain (2), apply (3), relate or compare (4), judge importance (5), create or design (6) |
| Knowledge type | Declarative · procedural · conditional | Knowing what, being able to do it, or knowing when and why to use which |
| Scope | Big-picture · detail | Would missing it break their understanding of the whole topic, or just one answer? |
| Stored or not | Stored, hard to reach · not stored | Stored: "oh yes, of course" once cued, or a tip-of-the-tongue feeling. Not stored: still blank or wrong after the cue (Tulving & Pearlstone, 1966). |

"Silly mistake" is never a classification. Find which piece of knowledge failed.

## The fix for each kind

The general rule is to re-learn, not reread. Go back to the material and learn the gap again, but connect it more deeply or make sense of it from a different angle than the first time. Rereading the answer only feels like fixing it.

| Gap | Fix |
|---|---|
| Big-picture or higher-level (Bloom 4–6), not stored | Rebuild that part of the map from the material, asking "why does this matter?", "how would I use this?", and "how does this connect to <other idea>?". Retest with questions that relate ideas or ask for a judgment. |
| Weakness across the whole topic, found in a later session | The first pass at learning it was thin. Rebuild the topic's structure from the material before more retrieval. |
| Stored but hard to reach | Practice with the right cue. Find how the knowledge will be used, then practice recall from that trigger: scenario problems if it's applied, question-and-answer only if that's how it's used (Morris, Bransford & Franks, 1977). |
| Procedural, incorrect or stuck | Re-learn the procedure from a worked example or walkthrough, then do it from memory again without cues. Then change the numbers, then add a condition. |
| Conditional (knew how, not when) | Mixed problem sets and edge cases that force choosing the approach (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007). |
| A lower-level detail that won't connect to anything | Spaced flashcards, as a last resort. |
| Correct but unsure | Treat it like the matching wrong answer at the same level. Retest a variation, not the same question. |

Then schedule the retest:

- The gap goes into the next spaced session (1 day, 1 week, or 1 month, whichever comes next).
- The retest uses a different technique from the one that found the gap, so it comes from a new angle.
- If the same gap survives two retests, change how it's being learned, not just how it's being tested.

## Gap log format

Terse, one line per gap, with the fix in parentheses:

```
Date: <M/D/YY> · <topic> · <stage> retrieval · techniques: <list>
- <concept> · <couldn't recall | incorrect | unsure> · Bloom <n> · <stored | not stored> (<fix>)
Next session: <date/stage> · <technique that retests these>
```

If the learner already keeps a log, match its format instead.

## Diagnosing the technique

- **Easy, and no gaps found.** The technique was aimed wrong. Unless the knowledge is truly complete, retrieval should find gaps. Usually it missed a level of thinking or a knowledge type. Raise the level, add edge cases, or add an outside check.
- **The learner struggled to run the technique.** Practice that technique for a few more sessions before adding more.

## Confidence

People with little knowledge of a topic tend to overrate their grasp of it (Kruger & Dunning, 1999). If a learner feels very sure of a topic they've studied only briefly, the feeling won't go away on request. Plan around it: assume hidden gaps and check against an outside source.
