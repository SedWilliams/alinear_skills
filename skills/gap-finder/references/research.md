# The research behind the rules

The rules in this skill come from the user's Learning Science notes in Notion (`learning-science.md`), which are the source of truth. This file adds published research on learning and memory that supports those rules. It gives the short version of each finding and how the skill uses it. Full references are at the end. Where a finding here and the notes differ, follow the notes. You don't need to cite these to the learner. Cite them when the learner asks why a rule exists.

## 1. Testing finds gaps and builds memory

Retrieving something from memory strengthens it more than restudying it does (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Dunlosky et al. (2013) reviewed ten common study techniques and rated two as high utility: practice testing and spacing practice out over time. A retrieval session does two jobs at once. It shows the learner what they can't recall yet, and it makes what they can recall stick.

Drawing a concept map from memory counts as retrieval. Blunt and Karpicke (2014) found it improved learning as much as writing out free recall did. Drawing a map with the material open is a study activity instead, and in an earlier study, retrieval practice beat it (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011).

## 2. An unsure right answer is a gap

Correct answers given with low confidence are fragile. Butler, Karpicke and Roediger (2008) found that feedback after a low-confidence correct answer improved how well learners kept it. So the skills log a correct but unsure answer as a gap (type "Shaky"), give brief feedback on it, and re-test a variation later.

## 3. Always answer, even with a guess

Trying to produce an answer, even a wrong one, and then getting corrected helps later recall more than reading the answer straight away (Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009). Generating information beats reading it (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). Errors made with high confidence get corrected especially well once the learner sees the right answer (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001). So the skills ask for a best guess instead of accepting "I don't know".

## 4. A session that finds nothing measured nothing

A test that every student aces can't tell what any of them knows. The same holds for one learner. If every answer comes back sure and correct, the questions sat below the level of what they know. Easy practice also misleads learners about how much they've learned (Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013), while practice that feels harder often improves long-term learning (Bjork, 1994). That's why gap-finder escalates after three sure, correct answers in a row.

## 5. Stored but hard to reach

Some memories are stored but can't be reached without the right cue. Tulving and Pearlstone (1966) found that category cues brought back words people had failed to recall on their own, so those words were available in memory but not accessible. Bjork and Bjork (1992) describe the same split as storage strength versus retrieval strength. The skills test it with a cue ladder. If a cue brings the answer back, the gap is "Locked" and the fix is practice with better cues. If even naming the idea doesn't help, the gap is "Missing" and the fix is learning it again.

## 6. Train the cue you'll face

Recall works best when the cue at test matches how the information was processed during learning (Tulving & Thomson, 1973; Morris, Bransford & Franks, 1977). If a student will need the knowledge while facing a problem, drilling question-and-answer flashcards trains the wrong cue. The skills ask how the learner will use the material and phrase questions the way the real situation will.

## 7. Big ideas first

Experts organize a field around its underlying principles, while novices group things by surface features. Chi, Feltovich and Glaser (1981) showed this with physics problems. A learner missing a big idea gets many detail questions wrong for one reason, so the skills test structure before details. Ausubel (1968) argued that new material is learned by attaching it to what the learner already knows, which is another reason to check the structure the details hang on.

## 8. Knowledge types

Declarative knowledge is knowing what (facts and concepts). Procedural knowledge is knowing how (carrying out a process). Conditional knowledge is knowing when and why to use which (Paris, Lipson & Wixson, 1983). Each needs different questions. Maths and code are mostly procedural, biology mostly declarative, and many subjects mix them. Conditional knowledge shows up best in problem sets that mix types, where the learner has to choose the method.

## 9. Levels of thinking (Bloom's)

The revised Bloom's taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) sorts thinking into six levels. The skills use it to make sure a session reaches the level the assessment needs.

| Level | Name | A question at this level asks the learner to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Remember | Recall a fact, term, or step |
| 2 | Understand | Explain it in their own words |
| 3 | Apply | Use it on a problem |
| 4 | Analyze | Compare ideas and say how they relate |
| 5 | Evaluate | Judge which idea or link matters most, and defend it |
| 6 | Create | Build something new with it, such as a hard question or an edge case |

## 10. Confidence and its limits

People with little knowledge of a field tend to overrate their skill in it (Kruger & Dunning, 1999). Learners also judge their own learning poorly right after studying and much better after a delay (Nelson & Dunlosky, 1991). So the skills take sure, unsure, and guess tags seriously, but treat "sure" on a lightly studied topic, or on material seen an hour ago, as a claim to test with a harder question.

## 11. Spacing

Spreading practice over time beats cramming it into one sitting, across hundreds of studies (Cepeda et al., 2006). The best gap between sessions grows with how long the material has to be remembered (Cepeda et al., 2008). The skills default to same day, next day, a week, and a month, then a full review in the weeks before an exam. That schedule is a convenient default, not a law. Gaps found in one session get re-tested in the next.

## 12. Mixing and varying

In the research sense, interleaving means mixing problem types within one practice set, so the learner has to pick the method for each problem. Mixed sets beat blocked practice on later maths tests (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007), and mixing examples from different categories improved category learning (Kornell & Bjork, 2008). The user's notes use "interleaving" more broadly, for reviewing from multiple angles, and they call for a different technique in each session. The studies above don't test that part directly. It comes from the notes. A learner who has adapted to one question format can pass it without knowing the material any better.

## 13. Explaining, teaching, and writing questions

Explaining material to yourself improves understanding (Chi et al., 1994). Asking "why?" about facts helps learners remember them, a technique Dunlosky et al. (2013) call elaborative interrogation. Teaching material to someone else helps the teacher learn it (Fiorella & Mayer, 2013). An explanation that falls apart shows the learner where the gap is.

Writing a good question about a topic takes understanding too. Maths educators use problem posing to see what students understand (Silver, 1994), and Brown and Walter (2005) describe a "what if not?" method that makes new problems by changing a problem's conditions. The skills use both. If writing a hard question is the struggle, the learner can't yet see what matters and why. If answering it is the struggle, they need practice applying it.

## 14. What doesn't find gaps

Rereading and highlighting feel productive and find almost nothing. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated both low in utility. Looking at the answer before trying skips the retrieval that does the work (section 3). The skills never recommend them as review.
