## 1. What counts as a gap

> Knowledge gaps are: 1. Any parts you struggle to recall 2. Any parts you get incorrect 3. Any parts you feel unconfident about. **Knowledge gaps are not only when you get things incorrect!**

> Always follow your sense of confidence; not the 'answer sheet'. Even if you get something technically correct, a lack of confidence with your answer is a legitimate gap. Confidence is more accurate than answers.

The fix for a gap is to re-encode it, using the same methods as the first time or new ones, but "try to connect the information more deeply or try to make sense of it from another perspective."

## 2. Knowledge types

Match the retrieval method to the knowledge type:
- **Declarative**: facts and concepts.
- **Procedural**: skills, carrying out a process.
- **Conditional**: when and why to apply declarative and procedural knowledge. It is built mainly by *integrative* practice, which uses the same knowledge in more varied ways.

One note also lists **temporal** knowledge alongside these. Deep processing and retrieval should both be planned around the subject's ratio of procedural to declarative knowledge. Maths and code are mostly procedural, biology mostly declarative, and English varies by task.

Mixed subjects: use declarative methods until declarative competence is good, then move to procedural methods, from lower order to higher. Predominantly procedural subjects start low and climb. There, lower-order competence gets tested directly by the higher-order methods.

## 3. Orders of learning (Bloom's)

| Level | Name | What it looks like (the user's annotations) | Who reaches it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | Create | New ideas, conjecture | PhD experts |
| 5 | Evaluate | Weigh, critique, prioritise: which relationships and concepts matter most, and why | Top 1% |
| 4 | Analyse | Compare and contrast concepts; how do they relate and connect? | Top 10% |
| 3 | Apply | Solve problems, including with other types of content | Top 10% |
| 2 | Understand | Explain, discuss, elaborate, paraphrase | Top 30% |
| 1 | Remember | List, describe, recall details; repetition | Barely pass |

In the Bear Hunter System, **Aim** gets the learner to about levels 3 and 4. **Skin** (consolidating relationships and chunk structure) gets them to level 5. A learner who has done Skin "should crush curveball questions."

## 4. Interleaving principles

> Review information from **multiple angles and perspectives** … It also protects you from *curveball* exam questions.

> An expert's "knowledge schema" is different to that of a novice. Experts have higher-order structures.

Key principles:
- Use **different** interleaving techniques for different retrieval sessions. Challenging knowledge the same way every time has limited benefit.
- Across the combination of techniques, cover the **full spectrum** of orders of learning and procedural needs.
- **Start with higher-order techniques** in the early sessions to find big-picture gaps. "These are more harmful when missed than gaps in small details."
- Lower-order techniques fill spare time or come in the later sessions.

To choose techniques: (1) decide how much lower-order versus higher-order retrieval is needed, (2) decide how procedural the needs are, (3) experiment with a technique that covers both.

## 5. Retrieval methods ranked by what they find

From the user's **Interleaving Table**.

### Declarative
- **3Cs (cover, copy, check), image-occlusion flashcards**: low-level fill-in-the-blank. Good for labeling diagrams.
- **Flashcards**, in three kinds. *Simple* cards test isolated facts and get overloaded when encoding is weak. *Simple relational* cards test how 2 or 3 concepts affect each other. *Evaluative* cards hold an evaluative question.
- **Generated questions**, in three kinds:
  - *Isolated*: single facts, mostly "what" questions.
  - *Simple relational* (two ideas) and *multi-relational* (more than two).
  - *Evaluative*: discuss the relationships between several ideas and **make a value judgment** about their strength and importance compared with other concepts. These have "significantly beneficial learning effects for depth of knowledge and retention." Create them in one session and answer them in another for two rounds of benefit.
- **Brain dumps**: *linear* dumps are poor at finding gaps, because you only stumble on them, and are useful just before an assessment. *Mindmap* dumps are "superior for higher-order knowledge testing" and recommended early, "to find big-picture gaps quickly."
- **Teaching** (to an imaginary student, who won't fill in your gaps for you):
  - *Isolated* teaching is lower-order and slow.
  - *Simple relational* teaching finds gaps quickly.
  - *Modified Whole-Part-Whole* is expert-level.
- **Practice questions**:
  - *Direct* (answer, then check) "increases the risk of missing gaps or classifying incorrect answers as 'silly mistakes'" because the root cause stays hidden.
  - *Extended*: (1) answer, (2) evaluate confidence, (3) create a perfect answer, (4) check it against the official answer. Any question the learner felt unsure about is a gap, "regardless of whether it was 'officially' correct."
- **Peer or group discussion (evaluative)**: has an explicit goal to critique and argue the importance of relationships.
- **Chunkmaps and GRINDE maps**: re-encoding old material as chunkmaps is a retrieval method. "Most people struggle to create a chunkmap with previously poorly encoded information."

### Procedural
- **Retrieved execution**: doing the skill from memory with no cues.
  - *Simple*: isolated, without variation. It is the first stage, and it becomes ineffective after mastery.
  - *Integrative*: combine several skills into a more complex function. Move here as soon as basic competency arrives, because this is what builds conditional knowledge.
  - *Applied*: work backwards from a target output, such as a real project or a real conversation scenario. The hardest and most effective form.
- **Challenges**: a prompt or problem stem to solve.
  - *Simple*.
  - *Integrative*: combine processes in ways that were never directly taught, such as multi-step maths or physics.
  - *Edge-case*: atypical uses in unfamiliar contexts. Solving one needs strong declarative knowledge of the principles. "Creating edge-case challenges is much easier than solving them," because creating one only requires knowing that concepts could influence each other.
- **Variable modification and addition**:
  - *Modification*: same problem, new numbers. Low-level.
  - *Addition*: add a variable that makes logical sense and still has a solution. The benefit comes from deciding which variable, whether it makes sense, what value it takes, whether a solution exists, and how it interacts with the existing variables. Examples from the notes: air resistance in mechanics, a surface-area constraint on cylinder volume, a time-out in a coding challenge, more speakers in a language dialogue.

The notes mention AI as a way to create harder variations of existing problems.

## 6. Self-made and premade questions

Good learners use both.
- **Premade** questions save time, show the style of the assessment, and find gaps "by testing yourself in ways you wouldn't have thought of."
- **Self-made** questions strengthen understanding from a different angle, find gaps in content already learned, and let you target the orders and types of retrieval you need. Over-reliance on premade questions is why curveballs throw students.

**Math SKIN diagnostic** (the user's own note): after a problem, create new questions that are "as hard as possible, not 'fill in the blank' but genuinely creative, relevant."
- If it is **hard to create** questions, that implies "a poor theoretical understanding of the importance, purpose, and relations": a flawed understanding of the schema.
- If it is **hard to answer** them, the learner needs more practice and new information: a flawed ability to apply the schema.

The user ranks ways of learning maths, from worst to best:
1. Watch, then attempt.
2. Theory, then formulae, then premade questions, then update the schema.
3. **Theory, then formulae, then create a practice problem and complete it, then update the schema.**

## 7. Cues and locked memories

Memory has two parts:
- **storage**: is the information in there?
- **access**: can a **cue** trigger it?

A memory that is stored but poorly connected to a cue is **locked**. "Many times, we think we have an issue with storage, but we actually have an issue with access." The two need different fixes.

Not every cue is equally valuable. If you only ever drill question-and-answer flashcards but never need the knowledge in that format, "too much training with this cue can make it harder to access the memory with more meaningful and realistic cues." To pick cues, ask: "In what situation **should** I be able to use this information? What **should** trigger my recall?" If the real use is decision-making, the cue is "being confronted with a relevant problem," so practise with scenarios.

## 8. Schema quality and where gaps hide

- **Connections beat effort.** "Effort spent developing a node or chunk does not necessarily correlate to increased retention." A node that isn't connected by relevance and importance to other nodes gets forgotten. Invest in highly connected nodes; they pull the outlying nodes along with them.
- **Ask why and how, not what.** "Why does this happen? Why is this important? How does this relate to everything else?"
- **Intuitive chunks** use prior knowledge, so they need less memorizing. A good map is "so intuitive that … remembering just one aspect/node of the map" brings the rest up. A map whose only route to recall is memorizing every chunk name is unintuitive.
- **Biggest, most important, axiomatic chunks first**; the details follow. "Some details *will* be out of reach."
- **Mind-map errors**, which mark where gaps are likely:
  - spiderwebbing
  - wheel and spoke
  - waterfalling
  - *detail stripped* (layers of detail are missing, usually from fear of overload)
  - cracked glass
  - no clear backbone
  - *single node chains* (one link in, one link out: weak points)
  - *lonely chunks* (few relationships)
  - *islands* (chunks built in isolation and connected artificially afterwards)
  - keyword anchoring needed
- **Reverse chunking**: if a detail repeats across several chunks, try a structure in which that repeated element becomes the chunk. Always weigh alternative structures; never assume the first one is best.
- **Order control**: don't process information in the order it was given. The learner should control the order and organization.
- **The user's own example**: a typography map split into separate typo/type/typeface/font nodes, and retention "was bad." A single typography chunk (typeface → font ← modifications) linked to prose, comfort and recognisability gave good retention.

## 9. Confidence calibration

- People are usually overconfident, especially where they know a little (Mt. Stupid on the Dunning-Kruger curve). High confidence happens at only two points: Mt. Stupid and real expertise. Quick check: *Do I feel very confident? Have I spent years becoming an expert?* A yes and then a no means the learner is probably overconfident.
- Adam Grant's question: "What conditions are necessary for my belief to be true?"
- The learner still *feels* confident; the job is to stop that feeling from steering behavior (the "wise mind" model). In a session, take sure/unsure/guess tags seriously, but treat "sure" on a lightly studied topic as a claim to test with a harder question.

## 10. Retrieval hygiene

1. Don't look at notes within 12 hours of a retrieval session. Doing so cues information back into working memory, so the session no longer tests long-term memory.
2. Always generate an answer, even when unsure. "Getting the wrong answer helps you remember the right answer when you correct yourself later."
3. Recall from memory without help first. Looking at notes or answers early "cheats yourself out of the learning process."
4. Use rote memorization with spaced repetition only as a last resort, for information that doesn't integrate into the knowledge network.
5. **External calibration.** A learner "may consistently miss blind spots … because you may not know enough about the topic to even realise that your testing scope is not comprehensive." At least one method should calibrate against a reliable outside source: "a formal answer, question bank, or even a reliable AI that is trained on your particular assessment scope." In a session, Claude is that source, which is why step 2 of the skill maps scope beyond the learner's notes.

Retrieval comes in three kinds:
- *planned*: scheduled sessions;
- *opportunistic*: real work that tests knowledge. Note which orders and knowledge types it tests, and fill the deficits in dedicated sessions;
- *micro-retrieval*: within minutes of learning, such as processing before writing notes, or teaching right after learning.

## 11. Spacing

- The rule of thumb is **same-day, 1 day, 1 week, 1 month**, followed loosely. Then cover everything for the assessment in the 1 to 4 weeks before it.
- Spacing that is too frequent creates needless repetition. Spacing that is too infrequent makes sessions overwhelming, because too much has been forgotten.
- Expanding gaps beat fixed intervals for workload, and they "expose knowledge gaps over a longer period of time away from the knowledge."
- With good encoding, about 80 to 90% of the material should be retained by the end-of-week session. Weak encoding has to be offset with more retrieval for now.
- Retrieval never drops to zero, because "our brain is never leak-proof."

## 12. Signs the retrieval itself is broken

From "How do I know if I am competent in this?":
- **The learner struggles to apply the methods**: practise the method more.
- **"You find it very easy but do not find any knowledge gaps"**: "It most likely indicates that the technique was performed incorrectly. Unless you truly have pristine knowledge, you should be able to find gaps. Retrieval techniques are meant to find gaps. Not finding any means it was ineffective." It probably "did not cover all the necessary orders of learning or types of knowledge testing."

**Target**: look for areas the learner feels less confident in or hasn't used before, and start with the weak areas, "even if it feels uncomfortable." The Retrieval page adds: "Most learners subconsciously avoid their learning weak points."

**Teach challenge**: "Explain why a concept is important **before** introducing any terminology," in lay terms, as if to a 10-year-old. "Don't just explain, teach! Change the order and approach."

**Learning efficiency** is total mastery and retention divided by total time, where partial mastery for each Bloom level is the retention after one week divided by the retention the assessment requires. A gap report that groups gaps by Bloom level shows the learner which partial mastery is lowest.
