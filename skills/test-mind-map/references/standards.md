# Map standards

## 1. What a map is for

Building a map helps learning when the learner does the selecting, organizing, and connecting (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016). Across studies, working with concept maps went with better retention than reading text, attending lectures, or class discussion (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006). A map copied from the material, or one Claude reorganizes, skips the learner's part of that work.

How the map was made changes what it shows:
- Drawn with the material open, a map is a study product. It shows how the learner organized the material, not what they can recall.
- Drawn from memory, a map is a test. Building a concept map from memory improved learning as much as writing out free recall (Blunt & Karpicke, 2014).

A map is doing its job when what's already on the page makes the next link easier to think of, when each part can be tied back to the big picture, and when the learner keeps revising it as they understand more.

## 2. Map shapes: spoke, chain, net

Kinchin, Hay and Adams (2000) sorted students' concept maps into three shapes:
- **Spoke.** Every idea links straight to the central topic, and no branch links to another. The learner has a list of facts about the topic but hasn't related them yet. Common, and fine in a first draft.
- **Chain.** Ideas form a line, one after another, often in the order a lecture or textbook gave them. Each link may be right, but few links cross the sequence.
- **Net.** Branches link to each other, so there's more than one route between ideas. This shape goes with deeper understanding.

A finished map should be moving toward a net. The script flags a spoke shape (no branch off the center links to another except through it), individual branches linked only through the center, and pass-through nodes, which are the links of a chain.

## 3. The backbone

The backbone is the few biggest, most general ideas that everything else hangs from, plus the main relationships between them. Concept maps put the most general, inclusive ideas at the top or center and the more specific ones further out (Novak & Gowin, 1984). Teaching sequences that start broad and add detail in layers follow the same logic (Reigeluth, 1999).

- Most topics have about 3 to 7 big ideas. Fewer can be right for a small topic. More usually means some are details.
- The big ideas should link to each other, not only to the center. A missing link between two big ideas costs more than a missing detail.
- Warning signs: no clear backbone, a backbone that is the source's section headings, or detail crowding the map while the big ideas are missing or vague.

## 4. Groupings

- Experts group by underlying principle, and novices group by surface features (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser, 1981). A strong group exists because of what makes its members matter: the problem they solve, the mechanism they share, the cause they have in common. A weak group exists because its members share a label, a heading, or a position in the source.
- Ask "why does this group matter?", then "so what?" until the question stops making sense. A group named for the deepest answer holds more, and links to other groups more easily, than one named for a nearby effect.
- An idea matters because of the problem it solves, not because of what it causes. "High-level languages matter because they make abstraction easier" names an effect. "Writing everything in a low-level language is slow, and the speed it buys often isn't worth the time" names the problem.
- **Rebuild test.** Cover the map, start from one big idea, and try to recall what hangs off it. In a well-grouped map, recalling one node brings back its neighbors through their relationships. If the only route is memorizing each node's name, the grouping isn't doing any work.
- A first structure is rarely the simplest. If one detail repeats under several groups, try a structure where that repeated detail becomes a group, and weigh both versions. Comparing structures is slow, hard work, and that's normal.
- There's no single right map. The best one fits what the learner already knows.

## 5. Links

- In a concept map, a link with words on it makes a statement, such as "light reactions → produce → ATP" (Novak & Cañas, 2008). A plain line only says two ideas are related, not how. Unlabeled links are the cheapest place to find gaps: ask what the link means.
- Arrows show which idea affects which. A link with no direction can't be checked for running backwards.
- Cross-links between branches show that the learner has connected separate parts of the topic, and Novak and Gowin (1984) gave valid cross-links the most points in their scoring scheme. Look for pairs of branches that obviously affect each other but have no link.
- A tangle is many point-to-point links between the details of two branches. Usually one relationship between the two branches explains them all. Finding it is the learner's job.

## 6. Branch counts and pass-through nodes

- Working memory holds about four chunks at once (Cowan, 2001), so a node with more than about four branches is hard to hold in mind. Ask the learner what the branches have in common and whether some are more alike than the others. Don't propose the subgroups.
- A pass-through node has one link in and one link out. It adds a step without adding structure. Often it can merge with a neighbor, or it's really a detail of the node before it, or it's the topic itself and belongs in the title. The learner decides which.
- A node at the edge of the map with no branches is fine.

## 7. Detail and layers

- An important detail helps the learner understand the idea it's attached to. Other details only have to be memorized. The test is whether knowing the detail helps you understand the concept it's attached to.
- **Detail first.** The details are there, but the big ideas are missing or vague. Structure should come first.
- **Too little detail.** A finished map with almost no detail skips the details the learner will have to recall, and it hides relationships that could challenge the structure. A first draft with little detail is fine.
- A detail can look like it only has to be memorized when the structure that explains it is missing. Lots of "just memorize it" detail can point to a weak backbone.
- Some maps tag layers. ALinear's are logic (the backbone), concept, detail, and arbitrary (memorize-only). Use the learner's tags when they exist. Don't invent them.
- Facts that connect to nothing can go to flashcards instead of the map.

## 8. Pictures and emphasis

Pairing words with images helps memory (Paivio, 1986), and drawing a symbol makes the learner decide what an idea looks like. Emphasis through size, color, or lettering forces choices about what matters most. Both help, but they rank last, after structure.

## 9. Standards by stage

| Stage | Expect | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| First draft | A few big ideas, guessed links, little detail | Lots of detail already, or groups copied from the source |
| Mid-study | Messy, being revised | Structure still copies the source's order; no cross-links forming |
| Finished | Clear backbone, labeled and directed links, cross-links, important details | A spoke or chain shape, unlabeled links, missing important details |
| From memory | The big ideas and their links | Missing or wrong ideas and links. Mark it with mind-map-dump. |

## 10. Gaps and retrieval

- A knowledge gap is anything the learner struggles to recall, gets wrong, or feels unsure about. A correct answer given with low confidence is still a gap (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2008).
- Always give an answer, even when unsure, before looking anything up. A wrong attempt that gets corrected helps memory (Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009).
- A session that finds no gaps was too easy, or it skipped a level of thinking or a type of knowledge.
- Self-testing misses blind spots the learner doesn't know exist. At least one check should compare against an outside source: the material, a question bank, or an answer key.
- **Stored or reachable.** If a hint or the name brings an idea back, it was stored but poorly cued, and the fix is linking it into the map so its relationships cue it. If even the name doesn't bring it back, it needs re-learning (Tulving & Pearlstone, 1966).
- Question levels for declarative knowledge, from lowest to highest: one fact, two ideas related, three or more related, and a judgment about which relationships matter most. For procedural knowledge: doing a single step, combining steps, working backwards from a goal, and edge cases.

## 11. Who does the thinking

The reviewer names problems and asks questions. The learner makes every decision about groups, links, and reasons. AI helps here by checking answers against the material and by writing questions. It does harm when it groups ideas or explains why they matter, because then the learner reads a structure instead of building one.
