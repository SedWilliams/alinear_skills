# Technique catalog

Every interleaving technique from the user's Notion page **Interleaving Table** (Area → Learning Science → SIR), with the order of learning it tests and when it earns its time. Orders use Bloom's levels: 1 Remember, 2 Understand, 3 Apply, 4 Analyze, 5 Evaluate, 6 Create. "Lower-order" means 1–2, "higher-order" means 4–6.

### Notes per technique

- **3Cs.** Cover part of the notes, rewrite or redraw it, uncover, check. Image-occlusion flashcards do the same thing more efficiently. Best for labelling diagrams and processes.
- **Simple flashcards** (Anki preferred). Good for definitions, constants, and details that don't fit relational notes. When higher-order encoding is weak, users overload them, and repetition plus steep forgetting curves waste a lot of time. Treat as a last resort for information that won't integrate.
- **Generated questions.** Creating a question is retrieval, and so is answering it, so splitting creation and answering across two sessions gets two rounds of benefit. Evaluative questions force a value judgment about which concept matters more. That judgment drives depth and retention.
- **Brain dumps.** Linear dumps are easy but poor at finding gaps: the user dumps until they stumble. Mindmap dumps test relationships and chunks, which harder assessments demand, so they are the higher-yield default early on. Move to lower-order methods afterwards. Building a concept map from memory improves learning about as much as writing out free recall (Blunt & Karpicke, 2014).
- **Teaching.** An imaginary student beats a friend, because a friend fills gaps with their own knowledge. When Claude plays the student, it must not fill gaps either. Teaching right after learning something (micro-retrieval) improves deep processing. Teaching challenge: explain why a concept matters before introducing any terminology, as if to a 10-year-old. Change the order and approach so it makes sense to someone who hasn't learned it. Don't just explain. Explaining to yourself (Chi et al., 1994) and teaching others (Fiorella & Mayer, 2013) both improve understanding.
- **Modified WPW.** The hardest technique in the program, and it needs near-total encoding mastery. Don't suggest it unless the user says they're at that level.
- **Direct practice questions.** Answer, check, review if wrong. This risks writing gaps off as "silly mistakes" and hides root causes. Practice questions are limited and valuable, so prefer the extended method.
- **Extended practice questions.** (1) Answer as well as possible. (2) Mark every answer the user felt unsure of. (3) Build a "perfect" answer sheet from the learning material. (4) Check that sheet against the official answers to find more gaps. Every unsure question is a gap, even when officially correct, because getting one version right doesn't guarantee getting variations right. Feedback after a low-confidence correct answer improves how well it's kept (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2008). This technique also does the external calibration.
- **Peer/group evaluative discussion.** Unlike open-ended group study, the goal is explicit: critique, judge, and argue the importance of relationships against the big picture. A debate format works: one side tries to show the other is missing a point.
- **Chunkmaps / GRINDE maps.** A Bear Hunter System encoding method, listed under retrieval because re-encoding older, poorly encoded content into chunkmaps lifts its mastery. Do it first, as early as possible, and leave time for other techniques. Expect it to be hard: the struggle comes from the original encoding never reaching higher levels.
- **Memorisation systems.** Strong for rote lists, but the use cases are narrow, they are lower-order only, and early reliance on them stunts proper encoding. Use them only for large, discrete, often ordered lists, and even then check for a better encoding.
- **Feynman technique.** Pick a topic, teach it to a child, find where the explanation breaks, return to the sources, simplify. Mostly superseded by the program's methods, but still useful as micro-retrieval: teach everything to a child straight after learning it.

## Procedural techniques

Procedural fields build on themselves: skills repeat naturally, and higher-order methods test lower-order competence directly. So the path is simple. Start low and climb.

| Technique | Bloom | Finds | Best stage | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ben system / simple flashcards | 1 | Vocabulary, syntax, isolated facts | Language vocab; otherwise rarely | Low |
| Retrieved execution, simple | 3 | Whether the user can run a single process from memory without cues | First practice of a new skill | Low |
| Retrieved execution, integrative | 3–4 | Combining several skills into one larger function (builds conditional knowledge) | As soon as simple execution is competent | Medium |
| Retrieved execution, applied | 4–6 | Planning backwards from a target output, then building it | Once integrative is comfortable | High |
| Challenges, simple | 3 | Applying one skill to a given problem stem | Early | Low |
| Challenges, integrative | 3–4 | Multi-step problems that combine processes in untaught ways | Mid | Medium |
| Challenges, edge-case | 4–6 | Applying skills to atypical, unfamiliar contexts (needs strong declarative knowledge) | Mid–late | High to solve, low to create |
| Mixed problem set (from research, not the notes) | 3–4 | Choosing the right method when the problem type isn't labeled | Mid | Medium |
| Variable modification | 3 | The same problem with new numbers | Early, low-level practice | Low |
| Variable addition | 4–5 | Whether an added condition makes sense, is solvable, and how it interacts with the others | Mid–late | Medium |

### Notes per technique

- **Simple retrieved execution** is the right first step. Kept up after mastery, it becomes repetition that no longer teaches anything. Move to integrative execution as soon as basic competence shows. Worked examples help beginners, and the help fades as skill grows (Kalyuga et al., 2003).
- **Integrative execution.** Coding: build bigger functions out of basic ones. Languages: chain simple phrases into dialogues.
- **Applied execution** works backwards from a goal. Coding: build an app for a real need, covering design, implementation, and QA. Languages: script variations of a real scenario, then live it. It is the hardest form of practice and the most effective.
- **Edge-case challenges.** Creating them is much easier than solving them, because creating only needs to know which concepts might influence each other. So the user can write edge cases as a retrieval exercise, and they can replace evaluative generated questions in procedural subjects. Claude can help generate harder variations of existing problems.
- **Mixed problem sets** come from research, not the notes. Shuffle the problem types so each problem starts with choosing the method. Mixed practice beat blocked practice on later maths tests (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007). This is what "interleaving" means in the research. The notes use the word more broadly, for reviewing from multiple angles across sessions. Use mixed sets as an add-on for conditional knowledge, never in place of the notes' techniques.
- **Variable addition.** The learning comes from deciding which variable to add, whether it makes sense, what value is reasonable, whether a solution still exists, and how it interacts with existing variables. Examples: add air resistance to a mechanics problem; add a surface-area constraint and a 1:2 radius-to-height ratio to a cylinder-volume problem; add time-outs, extra inputs, or further data transformations to a coding task; add speakers, viewpoints, or conditions to a language dialogue. Brown and Walter (2005) describe the same move as "what if not?" problem posing.

## Combination recipes

**Mostly procedural** (coding, maths-heavy courses, CSC assembly work). Start with variable modification and simple retrieved execution, move to integrative challenges and variable addition, then edge cases and applied execution. Skip extra declarative sessions once the concepts are solid.

**Mostly declarative** (biology, history, theory courses). The Notion page shows this recipe as an image. This version follows the written principles:
1. Early sessions: mindmap brain dump plus simple relational teaching, to find big-picture gaps.
2. Middle sessions: relational, then evaluative generated questions (create, answer next session), plus extended practice questions for calibration and assessment style.
3. Late sessions: evaluative discussion or flashcards, simple flashcards for leftover isolated details, and a linear brain dump right before the assessment.

**Mixed.** Build on the declarative recipe, because declarative knowledge usually takes longest to master. For procedurally heavy topics, swap in procedural techniques once declarative competence is good. Often that's after the first encoding plus one interleaved session. Then climb the procedural techniques from low to high order.

## Avoid

- Rereading, highlighting, and passive review of notes. They cue the material straight into working memory, so nothing is retrieved. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated rereading and highlighting low in utility.
- Grinding past papers with no target, the way "Sam" did in the Quick Learning System Check page: no idea how many to do, method copied from a friend.
- Copying any technique "because it works for others" without checking it fits the knowledge type and orders needed.
