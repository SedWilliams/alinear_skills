# Gap handling

Use this when the user brings gaps from an earlier session, such as a gap-finder report, a mind-map-dump report, or their own log, so the pick targets them. Logging gaps and writing the report are gap-finder's job.

From the user's Notion pages Knowledge Gaps, Retrieval, Basic Retrieval Flow (Target, Teach), How do I know if I am competent in this?, Locked Memories, Confidence in a topic, and How to Measure learning Efficiency. These pages are the source of truth. Research cited as (Author, year) supports them, with full references at the end.

## What counts as a gap

1. Anything the user **struggled to recall**: slow, partial, or needed a cue.
2. Anything they got **incorrect**.
3. Anything they felt **unconfident** about, even when the answer was correct.

Gaps aren't only wrong answers. Confidence beats the answer sheet: getting one version right doesn't mean the user will get variations right. Correct answers given without confidence are also less likely to come back later (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2008).

## Where to aim

- Start with areas the user feels least confident in or hasn't used before, even when that's uncomfortable. Most learners quietly avoid their weak points because it feels easier.
- Big-picture gaps before small details.
- Open gaps from the last log before new territory.
- Watch for blind spots. A user who doesn't know enough to see that their testing scope is incomplete can carry a gap through several sessions. External calibration catches this.

## Classify each gap

| Axis | Values | How to tell |
|---|---|---|
| Order | Bloom 1–6 | What the prompt demanded: recall a fact (1), explain (2), apply (3), relate/compare (4), judge importance (5), create/design (6) |
| Knowledge type | Declarative · procedural · conditional | Knowing what, being able to do it, or knowing when and why to use which |
| Scope | Big-picture · detail | Would missing it break the user's understanding of the whole topic, or just one answer? |
| Storage vs access | Storage · access | Access: "oh yes, of course" once cued, or the tip-of-the-tongue feeling. Storage: still blank or wrong after the cue (Tulving & Pearlstone, 1966) |

"Silly mistake" is never a classification. Find which piece of knowledge actually failed.

## The fix and the technique for each kind

The general rule: **re-encode, don't reread.** Go back to the material and re-encode the gap with the methods that encoded it the first time, but connect it more deeply or make sense of it from a different angle. Rereading the answer only feels like fixing it.

| Gap | Fix |
|---|---|
| Big-picture or higher-order (Bloom 4–6), storage | Re-encode the relationships: rebuild that part of the mindmap or chunkmap and ask "why is this important?", "how can I use this?", "how does this connect to <other chunk>?" Retest with relational or evaluative questions. |
| Topic-wide weakness found in a later session | The initial encoding was thin. Re-encode the topic as a chunkmap before more retrieval. |
| Access (stored but not reachable) | Train the right cue. Find how the knowledge will be used, then practise recall with that trigger: scenario problems if it's applied, Q&A only if Q&A is how it's used (Morris, Bransford & Franks, 1977). |
| Procedural, incorrect or stuck | Re-encode the procedure (worked example, video, or walkthrough), then retrieved execution again without cues. Then variable modification, then variable addition. |
| Conditional (knew how, not when) | Integrative challenges and edge cases that force choosing the approach. Mixed problem sets do the same job (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007). |
| Lower-order detail that won't fit the knowledge network | Spaced flashcards, as a last resort. |
| Correct but unsure | Treat it like the matching incorrect case at the same order. Retest a variation, not the same prompt. |

When picking the retest:

- The gap belongs in the next spaced session (1-day, 1-week, or 1-month, whichever comes next).
- That retest uses a **different technique** from the one that found the gap, so it's tested from a new angle.
- If the same gap survives two retests, change the encoding approach, not just the retrieval technique.

## Diagnosing the technique itself

From "How do I know if I am competent in this?":

- **Easy, and no gaps found.** The technique was performed wrong or aimed wrong. Unless the knowledge is truly pristine, retrieval should find gaps. Usually it missed an order of learning or a knowledge type. Raise the order, add edge cases, or add external calibration.
- **Struggling to apply the technique.** Spend a couple more weeks practising it before adding more. This prevents overload.
- Before moving on to new lessons, the user should have done all three steps of the basic flow (test, target, teach) at least once.

## Confidence and overconfidence

On the Dunning–Kruger curve, confidence is high in only two places: Mt. Stupid (little knowledge) and real expertise (years of intensive study). Two questions:

1. Do I feel very confident?
2. Have I spent years intensively learning this?

Yes, then no, means probably overconfident. The feeling won't go away, so let the rational mind drive behaviour: plan for hidden gaps and calibrate externally. Kruger and Dunning (1999) found the same pattern: people with little knowledge of a field tend to overrate their skill in it.

## Measuring learning efficiency (optional)

Learning efficiency = total mastery and retention ÷ total time spent learning.

1. Find the lowest and highest Bloom levels the assessment requires.
2. For each level, estimate retention after one week (%).
3. Set the retention the assessment requires for each level.
4. Partial mastery = actual one-week retention ÷ required retention.
5. Total mastery = average of the partial masteries.
6. Report it as "<total mastery>% over <hours> hours".

Example (Jeremy, 2nd-year economics, levels 1–4): retention 60/60/50/30 vs required 80/90/70/60 gives partial masteries of 75/67/71/50%, so 66% over 30 hours. The lowest partial mastery (level 4 here) shows which order to target next.

If doubling hours barely moves total mastery, the methods have hit their limit. More time with the same technique won't help; change the technique.
