# Probing holes

A hole is a hypothesis. The map suggests the learner doesn't understand something, and a question tests whether that's true. The answer, plus how confident the learner is, decides which holes are real gaps and which are only things the map left out. Section names in brackets point to `standards.md`.

## Holes and the questions that test them

Fill in the learner's node labels. Ask one question per turn. The "real gap if" column says what makes the hole real.

| Hole | Question that tests it | Real gap if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No clear backbone | "Without the map, what are the few big ideas this whole topic hangs on, and how does one lead to the next?" | They list the source's section titles, list more than about seven, or can't link them. |
| Big idea with no stated reason, or only a nearby effect | "Why does [idea] matter?" Then "So what?" until the question stops making sense [Groupings]. | The answer is an effect rather than the problem the idea solves, or the "so what?" chain dies after one step. |
| Group built on surface features or copied headings | "If the material had never used the heading [X], would you still group these together? What do they have in common that matters?" | They can only name a shared label or position, not a reason. |
| Unlabeled or plain-line link | "What does the link from [X] to [Y] mean? If [X] changed, what would happen to [Y]?" | They can't give a direction or mechanism, or they reverse it. |
| Pass-through node | "What does [node] add between [A] and [B]? What would be lost if [A] connected straight to [B]?" | They can't say what the node contributes. Then the structure question is theirs: merge it, move it, or remove it [Branch counts]. |
| More than four branches | "What do these [N] branches off [X] have in common? Are some more alike than the others?" | They see no differences among the branches, or only surface ones. Never propose the subgroups. |
| Spoke shape, or a branch linked only through the center | Pick the two unlinked branches most likely to be related. "How does [A] affect [B]?" | They find no relationship and can't reason toward one, or they offer a token one ("they're both part of the topic"). |
| Tangle | "Several links cross between points under [A] and points under [B]. Is there one relationship between [A] and [B] that explains them?" | They can only describe the point-to-point links one by one. |
| Detail first | "Which of these details could you drop and still explain the topic? Which concept does [detail] help you understand?" | They can't tell important details from memorize-only ones [Detail and layers]. |
| Too little detail, at a stage that needs it | "Take [branch]. What concepts and details sit under it that you'd need to answer an exam question on it?" | They can't produce them. That's a retrieval gap, not just a sparse map. |
| Grouping that doesn't help recall | The rebuild test. "Cover the map. Starting from [idea], what hangs off it?" | They recall names one at a time with nothing pulling the next one up, or they can't rebuild it [Groupings]. |
| Idea from the material missing from the map | Use the cue ladder below. | Depends on the rung where they recall it. |
| Link that looks wrong | "Walk me through why [X] leads to [Y]." | The explanation conflicts with the material. Say so plainly, cite the section, and ask them to rework the link. Don't supply the right mechanism. |
| Finished map with no transfer tested | Pose a situation the material didn't cover. "Using your map, what happens to [Y] if [new condition]?" | They can't use the map to reason about it. |
| Judgment | "Of [A], [B], and [C], which relationship matters most for [the topic's purpose], and why?" | They can't make or defend a judgment. |
| Plain explanation | "Explain [idea] to a ten-year-old. Start with why it matters, before using any of its terms." | They need the jargon to explain it, or can't say why it matters. |
| Procedural topic (maths, code) | "What problem does [method] solve? When would it fail?" Or an edge case: "Does [method] still work if [unusual condition]?" | They know the steps but not the problem, or they can't judge whether the method applies. |

Mix the levels. After two questions about how ideas relate, ask a judgment, explanation, or transfer question. Don't run five "how does X relate to Y" questions in a row.

## The cue ladder for missing ideas

For an idea from the material that's missing from the map, never name it first, because naming it is a cue. Climb one rung per turn:

1. **No cue.** "Your map covers [A], [B], and [C]. The material has another big idea that [drives B / sits between A and C]. What is it?"
2. **Partial cue.** Point to where it is in the material or what it does, without naming it.
3. **Named.** "Where does [X] fit on your map, and how does it connect?"

What the rung means [Gaps and retrieval]:
- **Rung 1.** They knew it, and the map just left it out.
- **Rung 2 or 3.** The memory is stored but poorly cued, a Locked gap. The fix is linking it into the map so its relationships cue it.
- **Not even at rung 3.** A Missing gap. They go back to the material and re-learn it.

## Reading the answer

- **Confident and right.** The hole was only an omission on the map. Say so in a line, specifically. Whether to add it to the map is the learner's call. Move to the next hole.
- **Right but unsure.** That's a Shaky gap, because confidence counts. Log it, then either ask one follow-up that firms it up or move on.
- **Partial or struggling.** Ask one narrower question on the same hole, without hinting at the answer. If it's a missing idea, climb the cue ladder. An idea that comes back is Locked, and one that doesn't is Missing.
- **Knows the nodes but can't relate them.** That's Unlinked. It's the most common real gap behind spoke shapes and branches linked only through the center.
- **Wrong.** That's a Wrong gap. Say it's wrong, specifically, and point to where the conflict is: the material's section, or your own knowledge if no material was given, labeled as yours. Ask one follow-up to find which link or assumption produced it, then ask them to rework it. Don't explain the right version.
- **"I don't know" twice, or shutting down.** Give a foothold (see SKILL.md), then go back to the question.

## Hints that give the answer away

A hint that contains the group, the link, or the reason does the learner's thinking for them [Who does the thinking].

| Gives it away | Keeps the thinking with the learner |
| --- | --- |
| "Could flour type, kneading, and salt go under 'structure'?" | "Which of these six branches are more alike than the others?" |
| "Isn't it important because it fixes slow development?" | "What problem existed before [X] was introduced?" |
| "Is the link that X increases Y?" | "If X doubled, what would happen to Y?" |
| "Try making 'Gas' its own group." | "What do the nodes feeding into [X] have in common?" |
| "Your map is missing [X]." | "The material has another big idea that drives [B]. What is it?" |

## Demonstrating a technique on a parallel example

Sometimes the learner can see the problem but not how to fix it, like how to regroup six branches or what to do with a pass-through node. Then show the technique on a different topic, narrate the reasoning, and ask them to apply it to their own map. Here's a ready one. Don't use it if their map is about baking.

- A map of bread baking had a "Factors" node with six branches: flour type, kneading, salt, yeast, water temperature, and proofing time. Asking "which of these are more alike?" split them in two. Flour type, kneading, and salt all build or tighten the gluten network, so they became "Structure". Yeast, water temperature, and proofing time all set how much gas forms, so they became "Gas". The split also exposed a cross-link the flat list had hidden. Gas only raises the loaf if the structure can trap it, so "Structure" got an arrow to "Gas" labeled "holds".
- The same map had "Kneading → Gluten develops → Dough gets stretchy". "Gluten develops" had one link in and one link out. Asking what it added showed that it and "Dough gets stretchy" described one change at two levels, so they merged into a single node, "stretchy gluten network", fed by kneading.

Never run the demonstration on the learner's own topic. That would be regrouping it for them.
