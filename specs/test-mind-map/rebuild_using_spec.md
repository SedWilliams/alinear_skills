## Mind maps and their components

Mind maps serve as a note-taking technique that rivals linear note taking strategies, like the Cornell note-taking method.

While linear methods have a 1:1 ratio of information written to amount of knowledge understood (assuming retention is perfect, which it is not), mind mapping greatly increases the amount knowledge understood (while greatly increasing retention and mastery) and reduces the information written leading to gains in efficency and mastery over material. This is possible because the drawn diagrams, relationships, and nodes represent much more than just what is written. They contain information regarding the connected nodes and their relations to each other, and they simultaneously act as memory cues which make all that information more recallable.
> Drawn from memory, a map is a test (retrieval). Building a concept map from memory improved learning as much as writing out free recall (Blunt & Karpicke, 2014).
 
<br>

The act of making the mind maps also engages higher levels of Bloom's taxonomy which increases the engagement of the learner while mapping, thus leading to higher retention and understanding.

The backbone is composed of the biggest, most general ideas that everything else hangs from. Plus the main relationships between them, which logically and intuitively lead from one backbone concept to the next (again, serving as memory cues). 
> Concept maps put the most general, inclusive ideas at the top or center and the more specific ones further out (Novak & Gowin, 1984). 
> Teaching sequences that start broad and add detail in layers follow the same logic (Reigeluth, 1999).

* The amount of logic chunks/nodes scales per subject covered/material density.
* The big ideas should link to each other, not only to the center. A missing link between two big ideas costs more than a missing detail.

### Reference Material

Material that is best memorized through rote-learning is specified as reference material and mind-maps should contain little to none of it.

These are extremely niche, and detailed, facts that form little to no connections and thus contribute more noise than value to the learner. These parts of the material are best left for reference manuals or cheat sheets, and they should either be left out of the mind map or noted as reference and seperated from the main map if written in the notes.

Reference Material is not to be confused with Details (see above) as details that help flesh out a concept are necessary and helpful to retaining that concept.

## Steadfast rules

A map copied from the material, or one Claude reorganizes, skips the learner's part of that work. **Do not support or engage in it.**
> Building a map helps learning when the learner does the selecting, organizing, and connecting    (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016). Across studies, working with concept maps went with better retention   than reading text, attending lectures, or class discussion (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006).

## Gaps and retrieval
- A knowledge gap is anything the learner struggles to recall, gets wrong, or feels unsure about. A correct answer given with low confidence is still a gap (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2008).
- Always give an answer, even when unsure, before looking anything up. A wrong attempt that gets corrected helps memory (Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009).
- A session that finds no gaps was too easy, or it skipped a level of thinking or a type of knowledge.
- Self-testing misses blind spots the learner doesn't know exist. At least one check should compare against an outside source: the material, a question bank, or an answer key.
- **Stored or reachable.** If a hint or the name brings an idea back, it was stored but poorly cued, and the fix is linking it into the map so its relationships cue it. If even the name doesn't bring it back, it needs re-learning (Tulving & Pearlstone, 1966).
- Question levels for declarative knowledge, from lowest to highest: one fact, two ideas related, three or more related, and a judgment about which relationships matter most. For procedural knowledge: doing a single step, combining steps, working backwards from a goal, and edge cases.

## Who does the thinking

The reviewer names problems and asks questions. The learner makes every decision about groups, links, and reasons. AI helps here by checking answers against the material and by writing questions. It does harm when it groups ideas or explains why they matter, because then the learner reads a structure instead of building one.
